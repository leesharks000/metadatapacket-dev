1. The innocent reading, in full

1.1 Classical Receptions Journal is the field's flagship. Since 2009 it has published work across a range no other classics journal matches.

1.2 Its geographic range is real. The abstracts alone receive antiquity in: - China: Dai Wangshu's Ovid, Lin Shu's Aesop, Luo Niansheng's Persae, two xiqu Antigones, Tacitus from the Jesuit libraries to the "Tacitus Trap"; - Vietnam (Phạm Duy Khiêm, "the first to venture into this intersection"); - Brazil (Basílio da Gama, Machado de Assis, Hilda Hilst); - Mexico, Puerto Rico, Turkey, Korea, Japan, Ukraine, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Russia, Israel, Portuguese India, Jewish Central Europe, Hellenistic Central Asia.

1.3 Its range of receivers is real too. Black diasporic reception is a sustained line: Baartman and Parks, Du Bois twice, Wheatley, McKay, Morrison, Walcott, Ijames. So are queer, feminist, disability and trans reception. It reads film, television, comics, video games, popular song and an online aesthetic. It has run special issues on Italian Fascism and Greek theatre, on failure in translation, on early modern poetics, on the First World War and on costume.

1.4 It examines how the field itself makes things. Silverblank and Ward ask how the "traditional" comes to seem pre-existing. Nisbet follows a fiction about Meleager through two centuries of public-facing scholarship. Braund reads the neglect of Vicars's Aeneid as class. Cox reads the silent reception of Kristina Chew's Georgics as an outsider's. Illarraga finds that an institution made Xenophon's cry quotable.

1.5 This is a working venue doing what a specialist journal does. The reading below accepts all of it. Its question is narrower: how standing moves through the journal, what kind of knowledge it is built to produce, who can use that knowledge, and what it does in the present.

2.2 The standard article. Six in ten (114 of 185) take a later work (a novel, play, film, translation, poem, painting, production) and read it against its ancient source. The receiving side is largely modern, with a contemporary skew: 119 of the 185 are set in the twentieth or twenty-first century, and 59% across all 389 coded works. The ancient side is held fixed (§2.6). The knowledge produced is an interpretation of a named maker's encounter with antiquity, and few are taken up: the 114 interpretations carry 121 citations between them.

2.3 Where the reception happens. - 72 of the 185 coded works are set in the twentieth century, 62 before 1900 and 47 in the twenty-first. - Of the 47 twenty-first-century receptions, eleven take up present politics: Islamophobia in Home Fire, settler extraction in Black Medea, propaganda in Miller's 300, necropolitics in Terzopoulos, the Greek bicentenary, the "Tacitus Trap" and others. - Media receptions number 21. Seven of them are films and series; the others are games, comics and a song. - The three digital ones are: - the drone's-eye view in digital cinema; - the Dark Academia aesthetic on Tumblr and its successors; - the webcomic Lore Olympus.

2.4 What does not appear. Across the 16,818 references, read for author, title and journal (corrected in v0.4, which reads the reference titles as well as the free text): - "algorithm" and "ChatGPT": none; - "artificial intelligence": twice, both in titles. One is a speculative essay whose subtitle lists artificial intelligence among its subjects (cited in Güthenke on exemplarity). The other is "Artificial Intelligence: Looking through the Pygmalion Lens" in AI & Society (cited in Swain on InSEXts, for Pygmalion). - "Google": twice, a news headline and a play's title (Stănescu, Google Me!); - "Wikipedia": once, an entry on the video game God of War.

No title or abstract takes a search engine, an encyclopedia platform, a recommender or a composing model as a receiver of antiquity. The platform enters once, as the medium of a subculture (Dark Academia), never as the receiver. That is a bounded census finding. Over the same years these systems became, for a growing share of readers, the first encounter with an ancient text. One interpretation followed, marked as one: the journal's working model of reception takes the receiver to be an author, and a composition layer offers no author to read. Tested against the referees' reports (§6.4.5), it is refined: the reports place the machine receiver under system evaluation, and one of them also names a route in which a reading of its composition could stand.

2.4.1 The open texts confirm it. None of the 37 open-licensed articles takes a search engine, a platform or a composing model as a receiver. - Artificial intelligence appears twice. In Budzowska's study of Polish theatre, it is a figure on stage: the Ajax production opens on an AI that rates the spectators' fitness for combat, and the article reads "Achilles' deified armour" as the Internet. In Lamers and Van Rooy, it is a future research tool for reaching the Neo-Greek corpus. - The digital otherwise enters as the audience's metrics: streams, YouTube views, Reddit and trending tags for Taylor Swift's "Cassandra", and a Steam achievement share for Assassin's Creed: Origins. - It also enters as "virtual antiquity" (VR and AR) in Leszczyk on Herbert. - In every case the machine is the medium or the scenery of an authored work. Nowhere is it a receiver whose compositions are read. - Of the 37, 24 engage no present condition of reception at all. The one that names a present market condition as the cause of a reception's fate is Cox on Chew: "Chew, on the other hand, had no previous books and no name for publishers to tout."

2.5 The one move toward production. Vandewalle (2025) sets aside "close readings of games as texts" and studies "the production processes and contexts of games set in the ancient world", interviewing developers. It is the register's clearest turn from the text of a reception to the conditions that produce it. It establishes, in its own words, "a new critical vocabulary". It has been cited twice.

2.6 The classics as the pre-inferential object. The reading so far left one term undistinguished. It is the classical object itself: the received text, its author, its date, its canon membership, its standing reading. In the vocabulary of the Critique of Pure Inference, this is a premise whose grounds the receiver need not reopen. Each of the 185 abstracts is coded for what it does with that object (codes-object.tsv):

2.6.1 The ten. They are: - Savani: a sixteenth-century woodcut, taken for a Roman wall painting, accepted uncritically for centuries. - Olive: the incest reading of Electra that scholars then "read into the ancient dramatic texts". - Mayer: the golden line, which "appears to have no basis in the classical tradition" and became a critical tool for reading the canon. - Silverblank and Ward: how the "traditional" comes to be accepted. - Gabriel: how the claim of Euripides' untimely modernity was built. - Possingham: the African influences on the Homeric tradition, "neglected or dismissed". - Loporcaro: the Latin under the founding text of Greek-first philology. - Kelleher: money under Plato's ontology, "more than he lets on". - Nisbet: Meleager's two books, "no evidentiary basis", two centuries durable. - Illarraga: the cry of the Ten Thousand, which "antiquity did not find quotable" and "an institution made so".

2.6.1.1 What the ten reopen. The ten cannot be sorted into changing the classic and changing a modern construction around it. The thesis of this frame is that modern constructions acquire the standing of the classic itself. The jealousy reading of Sappho 31 is such a construction (#1752). The useful question is which inherited determination each work reopens, and how far. - A reading read back into the text: Olive, the incest reading that scholars then "read into the ancient dramatic texts". This is the closest in the register to the operation #1752 traces: a modern reading becoming a property of the ancient object. - A claim about an author made constitutive: Gabriel, Euripides' untimely modernity, built by criticism from the Schlegels on. - A fact about a corpus: Nisbet, Meleager's two books. - A piece of evidence: Savani, the woodcut taken for a Roman painting. - A critical instrument: Mayer, the golden line. - A canonical tag: Illarraga, the cry of the Ten Thousand. - A line of influence: Possingham, Homer's African influences. - The discipline's founding premise: Loporcaro, the Latin under Wolf's Greek-first philology. - The text's own ground: Kelleher, exchange under Plato's ontology. - How tradition is made: Silverblank and Ward.

Each reopens a determination that the received classic had absorbed. Most reopen one locally: one reading, one fact, one image. None reopens the attribution, dating or standing reading of a canonical text as a whole (§2.6.2). These are the strongest exceptions, and they show where, and how far, the editorial layer permits an inherited determination to change.

2.6.2 What no abstract does. On the evidence of the abstracts, none questions the authorship or attribution of a canonical work, the boundaries between canonical authors' corpora, the received dating of a canonical text, or the standing reading of a canonical poem as such. Reception inside antiquity, where the received classic was itself made, has seven works. The classics are the object the field receives toward. They are almost never an object the field reopens.

2.6.3 What this does to the frame. The classical source enters as the result of earlier reception: attributed, dated, edited, classified and interpreted. Downstream it functions as a premise whose grounds need not be reopened. Those operations disappear into the object against which later receptions are read. The demonstrated pattern can be stated plainly: the journal's widening of contemporary reception proceeds largely with the inherited determinations of the classical object held as premises. Contemporary receptions multiply around the source while the earlier settlement keeps its authority. The proposition of §7.10, that the field cannot fundamentally shift deep reception, is a further claim about capacity. The pattern supports it. The strongest exceptions (§2.6.1.1) are where it is tested. The archive's own programme is the inverse case, and it sits outside the register on every count: - authorship reopened (Socrates as Orthonym; the Aristotle–Theophrastus corpus as one corpus divided by name); - dating reopened (Revelation first); - the standing reading of a canonical poem reopened (Sappho 31's jealousy reading, made by its editors, #1752); - ancient reception read as the making of the classic (The Production of Readers, #1767).

The coding is one reader's and revisable (§9.8). It is checked against the 37 open texts in §9.10.

How closed that is needs a baseline: the same counts in the comparison journals (§9.2).

3.2 Outward: what the field takes up. Crossref records 782 citations for the 405 works, and 184 works have none. - The single most-cited work is Charles Martindale's "Reception — a new humanism? Receptivity, pedagogy, the transhistorical" (2013): 45 citations. - Martindale's paper opened a forum: responses by Güthenke, Elsner, Rood, Porter, Friedman, Hardie and Easterling, his reply, an editor's note by Hardwick, and a bibliography of Martindale's publications on reception. - The 28 titles that address the field as such carry 138 citations, about five each. The 114 interpretations carry about one each. These two groups differ in age, though: most of the field titles date from 2009–2018 and the interpretations from 2019 on. - Matched by period, within the coded abstracts: - 2019–2021: theory and method pieces average 5.3 citations (8 works, 42 citations), interpretations 2.2 (38 works, 84 citations). - 2022–2024: theory and method pieces average 1.7 (6 works, 10), interpretations 0.8 (45 works, 35).

Matched, the gap is about two to one. Uptake goes more readily to the pieces in which the field states what reception is. The case studies, most of what the journal publishes, are taken up less. The difference is real and smaller than the raw totals suggest.

3.3 Who writes. Crossref holds affiliations for only 127 of 438 author records, all in recent volumes. Those it holds are led by the University of California, Oxford, Harvard, the Open University, Exeter, King's College London, Yale, Princeton and Cambridge. Repeat authors are few (32 with more than one work). Lorna Hardwick has the most entries (five). The editor has one article in the journal ("Postcolonial Sparagmos", 2015, cited 7) and is named in thirteen of its references.

3.4 The pattern of standing, stated as mechanics. The references and the uptake record a pattern. The founders and the journal are what the journal's articles cite most. The pieces that state what reception is are taken up more often. Most works, as case studies, are taken up little. The pattern is an observation of reference and uptake; a rule of admission would need evidence of submissions, decisions or comparative treatment. The archive holds one decision (§6.2) and one instance of uptake: the author's own conceptual development in the journal, "metatextual reception" (2014), with no recorded event of uptake in the journal's references or in Crossref.

The communities whose receptions the journal studies can, with two exceptions, read the articles about them only through a subscription or not at all. Whether a particular reader can reach a particular work through a library is beyond this audit. - the open 38 include the Fascism special issue, Ibsen, Shakespeare and Plautus, Wolf and Quintilian, Blake, Trollope and Taylor Swift; - the fifteen cite the journal itself 12 times in 847 references (1.4%), against 0.8% across the journal's works since 2019. The counts are small and are recorded as small. In them the margin's articles cite the centre's journal at nearly twice the journal's own rate.

4.2.1 The direction of the flow. The author (14 November, 19:01–19:02): the widening "is not a dispersal of semantic wealth outward"; "that is an appropriation of semantic wealth inward from the colonial margin". The margin supplies the receptions; the centre keeps the object they are read against (§2.6), the articles (§4.1–4.2), the citations (§3.1) and the standing (§4.3). The postcolonial widening is real as content. As an economy of meaning, it moves wealth from the margin into the centre and returns the result to the margin at a price. The alignment that makes a receiving work legible to the journal is the journal's operation, performed on the work (§7.10.1).

4.3 Who gains. - Authors gain credentials: the register lists twenty funded works (Leverhulme, the British Academy, the AHRC, Horizon 2020, national science and social science funds), and an article in the flagship counts toward hiring and promotion. - The press gains subscription revenue. - The field gains the standing of a journal of record. - The journal's own guidelines offer authors a pre-submission check for language and technical compliance: free as a report, with an edited file "for a one-time fee of $29" (the /non entry, from Paperpal's page for the journal).

These are the recorded gains. Who else benefits from the knowledge, and how far it reaches past its producers, is the question the uptake counts begin to answer and do not settle.

5. To what effect

5.1 Uptake. Across the journal, a work averages under two Crossref citations. Of the works from 2021 to 2025, 77 of 123 have none so far. Some of that is time lag. Beyond Crossref (§9.3), Semantic Scholar counts 1,249 citations for the 355 works it finds, against Crossref's 761 for the same works. The count rises and the shape holds: - The top. Martindale's "Reception — a new humanism?" leads with 89. - The gap, matched by period. 7. The journal as apparatus: a reception history

7.0 The innocent reading, in full. A journal exists so that work can be circulated, registered under its maker's name, judged by competent readers and kept. Each of those functions answers a real need. The procedures that serve them were built because the alternatives were worse: the patron's whim, the lone editor's prejudice, the printer's market. Most of what passes through them is improved, or at least preserved. The account below accepts all of this. Its question is what the procedure does to standing, and how the procedure itself has been received, built and capitalized.

7.1 The claim. The academic journal has always been a mediating procedure: one that governs admission and standing, and whose outputs shape its next inputs. "Algorithm" is used here in that extended sense. The procedure is not fixed, and its parts differ in kind: committees deliberate, referees give reasons, metrics aggregate, answer engines generate representations. Those differences determine where an objection can enter and what could change the result. The composition layer did not introduce procedural mediation into scholarship. It is the newest stage, and the first that composes its output in prose for whoever asks.

7.1.1 Three kinds of connection. The stations below recur in operation: each selects, authorizes, preserves and allocates. The recurrence supports comparison. It does not by itself establish one line of institutional descent from royal acquisition to generated answers. Each link is marked by kind: - documented transmission, where one stage's practice or text passes demonstrably into the next (the Royal Society's committee to its referees; the impact factor into the answer engine's card); - appropriation of a name, where a later institution takes an earlier one's name and emblem (Maxwell's Pergamon, the archive's Pergamon); - functional comparison, where two stations perform the same operation without a documented line between them (the Attalid acquisition and the composition layer).

7.2 Pergamon I: the acquisition apparatus. The name begins with the library of the Attalid kings at Pergamon, modelled on Alexandria's and competing with it. - The market for names. Galen dates a class of false attribution to that competition. Before the kings in Alexandria and Pergamum came to vie for old books, no treatise had yet been falsely titled; once sellers were paid, "πολλὰ ψευδῶς ἐπιγράφοντες ἐκόμιζον" (they brought many under false titles) (In Hipp. Nat. Hom., the seat galen; Who Benefits from Bad Inference?, draft, §3.1). The first collecting institution priced the attribution, and the supply answered with the name. - The corpus that hid from it. Strabo XIII.1.54: the heirs of Neleus at Scepsis, learning of the Attalid search for books, hid Aristotle's and Theophrastus's books in a trench. The Pergamon Counter-Archive (#165) reads the episode as a transmission dialectic: "concealment preserves but damages; institutionalization transmits but recomposes."

7.3 The society's procedure: 1665, 1752, the 1830s. - 1665. The Royal Society's Philosophical Transactions circulated research under the Society's name. The Pergamon Reclamation (#32) calls the mode a gift: "The scholar gave the work. The publisher gave it form. The library gave it shelter." - 1752. The Society replaced its single editor with a committee. In the Royal Society's own history, Aileen Fyfe writes that this "avoided the problem of trusting the institutional reputation to the failings or prejudices of any one individual." Collective editorial responsibility reduced "the reputational and financial risk of poor decisions", and it "created the impression of collective decision-making by a community of scholars" (Moxham and Fyfe; Fyfe, St Andrews). - The 1830s. The Society began asking referees for written reports. - Quality control. By Fyfe's account, the link between refereeing and quality control "did not emerge until the mid-twentieth century."

The procedure's early functions included protecting the institution's reputation, making the decision the institution's rather than any one person's, conferring a badge on those who passed, and guarding the Society's pages and plates. Judging the work was there from the start. Fyfe records that nineteenth-century referees "did point out logical flaws or errors of fact" and were "likely to comment on inadequate acknowledgement of the work of previous scholars". The Royal Society's own account ties the referees to the need for expertise and scrutiny the committee alone lacked. What came later, by her account, is the modern association of refereeing with "quality control". Substantive judgment and institutional protection ran together from the beginning. The question in any one decision is how they interact, which takes precedence, and who bears the consequences (§6.4.4). The anonymous referee and the editor who signs for "the journal" descend from that design. The ruling recorded in §0.4 inverts it: the decision of 7 November is the editor's to account for.

7.4 Pergamon II: the capitalized channel. In 1951 Robert Maxwell renamed Butterworth-Springer Pergamon Press, "after an ancient Greek city known for its library", and took a coin of that city for the logo (#32). He set up his headquarters at Headington Hill Hall, Oxford, the city of the press that publishes CRJ. The Pergamon Reclamation names five mechanisms: - Channel capture. In Maxwell's own boast, "a perpetual financing machine", owning "a series of publishing monopolies". - Title proliferation as enclosure. From 40 journals to more than 700. "International Journal of" was a favoured prefix, which a former vice-president called a "PR trick". - Editor capture. "He knew just what to offer to buy a person — fame or money." - Labour extraction without reciprocity. Unpaid authors and reviewers; institutions buying back their own employees' research. - Metrics as the translation of meaning into score.

The procedure of 1752 became a business. The standing it confers became the product, and the publisher's margin was the rent on the channel through which standing passes.

7.5 The index: standing as a number. Garfield proposed citation indexing in 1955. The Science Citation Index followed in 1964 and the journal impact factor in 1975: "a technology of valuation masquerading as a technology of retrieval" (#32). On 14 November the composition layer introduced CRJ to a reader through exactly this number: "2025 Impact Factor: 0.4; 2025 CiteScore: 0.8" (v12.681). The machine's first sentence about the journal is the index's.

7.6 The university as operator stack. The Capital Operator Stack and the University (#280, January 2026) maps the platform operators onto scholarly production: ranking through citation metrics, relevance through fundability, safety through conservative review, legibility through disciplinary gatekeeping, utility through impact mandates, access through credentials and paywalls. Its tautology: "Scholarship is that which has passed through systems whose primary output is the category 'scholarship.'" The Evaluator Exists (#741) gives the history of the proxies. Each "began as a reasonable heuristic" for triage when direct evaluation did not scale, and each "calcified into a gatekeeping mechanism." The paper names the inversion: lower-resolution proxies decide what higher-resolution evaluators may assess. The CRJ reports of §6.4 are that inversion at work.

7.7 The composition layer as the procedure's newest stage. Algorithmic Publishing Is Not Self-Publishing (#834) names the event: a composition system selects and delivers a work's substance to an audience through a generated answer. Read through this history, the answer engine applies the journal's oldest operations at the scale of a public: - it registers a work under a name (and can move the name: §6.2); - it certifies standing (the impact factor, the editor line, "foundational scholarship"); - it shelters some records and not others (the source dropped from the citations, v12.681).

The 1752 committee made the decision the institution's so that no individual's failings would touch the reputation. The composition layer makes the credit the institution's, so that no maker's name needs to appear.

7.7.1 How the stages compound (from §7.5 to §7.7). The answer engine does not meet an institution fresh. It meets one already represented through the earlier stations' determinations: an impact factor, a publication history, an editor's credential, a Wikipedia entry. It carries those determinations into its prose, and then adds one of its own, such as assigning the archive's programme to the journal. That is how the composition layer can extend an existing allocation of authority while changing its attribution. The authority it inherits is the journal's; the attribution it adds is new; the maker loses at the join.

7.7.2 The answer engine is taking on the journal's role. The author's formulation (14 November): "the composition layer is exactly like the academic journals, and taking on their role." The journal's four functions in the innocent reading (§7.0) were circulating work, registering it under its maker's name, judging it, and keeping it. The answer engine now performs all four for the public, at the point of first encounter: - Circulation. It circulates what it composes. - Registration. It registers work under a name: it gave the archive's programme to the journal on 10 November, and gave the term to its maker on 14 November. - Judgment. It judges standing: "the largest contributions … from interdisciplinary teams", "the specific coiners of that exact terminology". - Keeping. It keeps some sources in its citations and drops others.

It does this with the journal's own outputs as inputs: the impact factor, the editor line, the special issues. So it takes on the role while inheriting the journal's determinations. Its outcomes are returned in the journal's categories: mainstream and independent, peer-reviewed and experimental, institution and term. One difference keeps the comparison exact. The journal gives reasons that can be answered at the point of decision. The answer engine composes without them, and it can be answered only through a later re-run (§9.6).

7.7.3 The successor apparatus. The composition layer is taking over the journal's role while the journal evaluates it as a technical system. The journal's earlier editorial decisions become inputs to a successor editorial process. The CRJ case shows the transfer: - CRJ declined the manuscript. - The composition layer then circulated the programme and credited it to CRJ. - The journal controls admission to its pages. The composition layer controls another encounter, one in which the journal itself becomes an attributed author of scholarship. The journal's institutional identity is now subject to the same mediation it applies to others.

This changes what the declined submission was. It studied a competing, and increasingly consequential, apparatus of scholarly publication and judgment. That apparatus can: - publish an account without a submission; - assign credit without authorization; - supply readers with a disciplinary settlement before they meet any article.

It joins the demand argument of Who Benefits from Bad Inference? (§9 there). The composition layer can supply both the scholarship people encounter and the account of which scholarship they should want. Aligned with inherited academic standing, it can reproduce the journal's limits while absorbing the journal's public function. The journal is being received, edited and assigned standing by the kind of apparatus it already is.

7.10 The editorial layer of reception. The author's proposition (14 November): the journal "is not a field that can fundamentally shift any deep reception. its more an editorial layer keeping contemporary reception in alignment with earlier reception." The frame's evidence bears on it at four points: - The standard article is an alignment test. 114 of 185 works read a later work against its ancient source (§2.2). The new reception is measured by its relation to the source, and through the source to the reception history already settled around it. - The repertoire runs backward. Recovery is the journal's core operation (35 of 37 open texts, §5.3). It restores makers to an established history. It is rarely turned on a reception that might change how the source itself is read. - The new receiver is placed under the old terms. The reports put the machine receiver under system evaluation and received a philological reading of its composition as an empirical claim lacking controls (§6.4.5). The one submission in the record that argued a contemporary receiver could change how a fragment is read, by closing it, was declined. - Standing goes to the field's statements about itself. Matched by period, those statements draw about twice the uptake of case studies (§3.2).

On this reading the journal is an editorial layer in the sense of §7. Like the 1752 committee and the answer engine, it governs what enters the record of reception, and it keeps the contemporary aligned with the received. The innocent reading stands beside it (§1): the journal's global, diasporic and queer widening is real, and it widens who is read. The author adds (14 November): CRJ "is... forward thinking, among classics journals. but they froze what that means at one point in time, and there dont seem to be mechanisms for updating it." The register bears this out in three ways: - The forward edge is the founding one. Reception as a field, its widening scope, Martindale's "new humanism": the most cited work, the most referenced names and the forum of 2013 all date from the journal's first five years. Its mechanisms of self-revision on record are the 2013 forum and the special issues (anachronism, 2020; failure, 2022). Both debate how reception is to be read. Neither revises what counts as its object. - Corrections are individual. The register's only corrections are eight notices to individual articles. - No route for revising its terms. There is no visible route by which a new receiver, such as the composition layer, could change the journal's working definition of reception.

So the journal was forward at its founding and is fixed at that point. The proposition locates the limit elsewhere. A wider range of receivers is aligned with the same received sources by the same operation. A reception that would shift the source's own standing, such as Sappho 31 as a poem that makes its future reader, or a fragment closed by a machine, has no route through that operation. The strongest rival is the field's founding premise. That premise is that reception changes the meaning of the source. Martindale's Redeeming the Text (1993) is the field's charter. The journal marked its twentieth year with an editor's note by Hardwick and responses by Rood ("Redeeming Xenophon") and Hardie (2013). The register keeps the premise in practice: - Ranger proposes "a new reading of the conclusion of Ovid's episode" from Ali Smith's novel. - Greenwood's Laonikos "sheds new light on Thucydides' own reduplication". - Kachuck "puts the bear back in Horace". - Novokhatko's reading of HBO's Rome "opens new opportunities both for interpreting Lucan's text" and its reception. - Possingham reads Walcott toward "a new appreciation of the Homeric poem". - Papathanasopoulou's Graham "brings fresh illumination to our interpretation of the Oedipus myth". - Todorović's Bahanalii turns the Bacchae against Said's reading.

So the journal does print rereadings of sources through their receptions. The proposition therefore turns on "deep". The test has to keep three questions apart. Requiring the third to recognize the first would make institutional uptake the certification of intellectual depth, which is the allocation this frame studies. It would also fail the archive's own work, whose challenges to received objects have not acquired the standing to change their public reception. 1. Argument. Does the work propose a consequential revision to the received object or its governing interpretation? The abstracts and open texts are evidence for this. 2. Editorial treatment. How does the venue assess and accommodate that revision? This needs editorial evidence: the reports, the decision, the revisions asked for. The archive holds it for one submission (§6.4). 3. Subsequent reception. Does the revision alter what later readers meet as settled, in handbooks, classrooms and answer engines? This needs later reception history.

§9.10 runs the first question over the register. The second and third are recorded separately wherever evidence exists, and never folded into the first.

7.11.2 It files the making as one more reception. In the journal's own structure, the making of the classical object sits in the column of things received, beside Ali Smith and Fallout: New Vegas. The source column of the 141 works that hold the source fixed (§9.10) goes on holding Homer, Sophocles, Ovid and Vergil as the classical object. None of the seven turns its account of the making into a revision of what the source column holds. Gabriel, who shows a received view of an ancient author built by German criticism around 1800, comes nearest (§9.10, governing). Even there the revision stays with the one claim.

7.11.3 The colonial structure. The postcolonial and diasporic widening is one of the journal's real achievements (§1.3), and it is the editor's own field. It also makes the structure plain. Vietnamese, Brazilian, Caribbean, Indigenous Australian and Black diasporic receivers enter as responses to the canon. The canon stays at the centre, as the thing responded to, and they are read against it (source → later work in every case, §9.10). The arrangement is centre and periphery, with the centre's own making unexamined. With one exception, the receivers' own communities can reach the articles only through a subscription (§4.2).

7.11.4 The stewardship failure. The field's founding premise, that the object is partly constituted by its reception, licenses one move above all. It is to treat the "originary" object as itself the residue of a reception, that of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and to read the canon at the centre as that residue. The author (15 November, 13:21): "the fetishized philological object does more consolidating in the 19th". The field holds the instruments for this (§5.2–5.3) and the knowledge (§7.11.1), and keeps the residue as its fixed premise (§2.6). That is the failure of stewardship, stated as mechanics. The premise that founded the field is applied to everything the field studies, and never to the object that organizes the study.

7.11.5 A historical baseline (a line of research; the argument of §7.11.2–7.11.4 does not depend on it). §7.11.2 carries the claim: the making of the classical object is published as one reception among others while its results organize the remaining articles as settled premises. The baseline below asks a larger question, the author's (14 November): "when is the last time classics as such, as a discipline, actually incorporated a major revision of a fundamental object boundary into its ontology?" It is held as a line of research in the hologram (§0.6), to be checked against sources. On a first reading of the discipline's history, the last fundamental revisions of the classical object that classics incorporated were forced by evidence from outside the settlement of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: - the decipherment of Linear B (1952; Documents in Mycenaean Greek, 1956), which moved the boundary of Greek back by half a millennium; - the oral-traditional Homer of Parry (1928) and Lord (The Singer of Tales, 1960), established once fieldwork with living singers backed it; - the Near Eastern frame, from the Hittite Kumarbi texts (1940s) to Burkert (1984) and West (1997), absorbed slowly.

Later work adds within the existing categories: new papyri (Derveni, 1962; the Cologne Archilochus, 1974; the new Sappho poems, 2004 and 2014), a longer period (late antiquity), and a subfield (reception, after Martindale 1993). Reinterpretations of the corpus the discipline already held, however consequential, have become positions inside the field (Bernal's Black Athena, 1987, among them). They have not become revisions of its object. On that baseline, the composition layer is the first new body of outside evidence in decades about how the classics are received. It is the kind of evidence the discipline has historically let in, and its flagship reception journal has declined it (§6.4).

7.11.6.2 The innocent reading, in full. The article proposes sparagmos as a new term in "the lexicon of classical reception theory" (p. 2). The term carries violence, fertility and dislocation. Its completion is compositio membrorum, the gathering of limbs, which offers "a new version" of what was torn (p. 8). - Morrison tears Sophocles' Ajax apart and scatters it through Sula: in Shadrack, National Suicide Day, the man called Ajax, Sula as scapegoat, and the early death of the protagonist. - Soyinka places the sparagmos in the action of his play and in his critique of Nigerian power. He doubles Dionysus with Ogun and Christ and ends in a communion. - In both, the violence is regenerative. The article keeps "the contested history of the term and its potential evocations of racist violence" (p. 5) in view and turns the term toward "a new rite of anti-racist sparagmos."

It is a well-documented, widely cited article that gives the active force to Black and African writers.

7.11.6.3 The criteria. Five criteria are applied to the article. They are stated here so that any reading of it, by a person or a composer, can be checked against the text and the receiving works. - K1, where the colonizing act is placed: in the classical texts, or in the apparatus that composed them (editing, canon, origin claims). - K2, the point of contact: whether the reading finds the places where the receiving works recognize the discipline's colonization of the classics, and records them as the works' own claims. - K3, whose categories measure: the receiving text's or the composed classic's. - K4, the status of the source: whole, or already composed. - K5, who does the tearing.

7.11.6.4 The results.

K1. The article assigns colonial agency in four ways: - to the literature: postcolonial writing "breaks down the colonial accretions that this literature acquired as imperialists used it" (p. 7); the classical work is "imbued with the imprint of Western imperialism" (p. 20); Morrison's approach to "the canonical literature of Europe is a response to the historical violence and oppression that colonial Europe enacted" (p. 18); - to a "European canon" (p. 28); - to the imperialists who used the literature (p. 7); - once, in a note, to a theatre director (n. 5, Steadman, after Fischer-Lichte).

The agency is distributed with care. Users act (the imperialists who "used" the literature), and the texts bear the consequences ("accretions" attached to them). What the argument never examines as an agent is the apparatus that made the classical object: its editing, classification, canon formation and teaching.

Following the argument across its vocabulary, rather than counting one word: - The word "discipline" occurs nowhere in the text (0), and neither does "philology", "curriculum" or "edition" in the sense of a text edited (0). - The discipline is present, though, as authority. Frazer's Golden Bough (four times), Harrison's Prolegomena, Nietzsche, Hölderlin, Murray's translation, Wilamowitz and the scholia supply the metaphor's terms: sparagmos and fertility, the dying god, the Dionysiac, the structural problem of Ajax. - The nineteenth- and early twentieth-century apparatus enters the article a dozen times. Each time it is a resource the argument draws its frame from. None of those times is it examined as a maker of the object. - Schooling appears once, in Soyinka's own words: "my shock as a student of literature and drama when I read that drama originated in Greece" (p. 21). The receiving author names the classroom as the place where the claim reached him.

Scholars appear twice: as the agents the article sets aside ("not by scholars but by creative artists", p. 5), and as the Classicist who "might suppose" Caribbean classical names incongruous (p. 13). One party in the article is said to be owed no sympathy. Morrison's re-membering follows "a necessary dismemberment of classical literature which requires none of our sympathy" (p. 9). On the author's thesis, that party is the most colonized.

K2. Six points of contact, and what the article does with each: - a. Soyinka on the origin of drama. "I read that drama originated in Greece. What is this? … I never heard my grandfather talk about Greeks invading Yorubaland … I think the presence of eradication had better begin" (Appiah 1988, quoted p. 21). This disputes the discipline's claim about where its object came from. - The article quotes it to rebut the charge that Soyinka is "unduly influenced by European traditions", then lists the characteristics Greek and African theatre share (Budelmann). - The dispute with the discipline becomes evidence of the playwright's authenticity. - b. The slaves in the play. The opening stage direction gives a road "lined by the bodies of crucified slaves" (play, p. 1, quoted in Quaicoe). The Slave Leader calls: "Slaves, helots, the near and distant dispossessed!" (p. 7, quoted in Quaicoe). - The helots were the Spartans' enslaved population. The play restores to the Greek play the slavery of its own world. - The article reads the slave chorus as evoking "the colonial history that preceded Nigeria's independence" (p. 27) and the Nigeria of the 1960s. It cites neither the stage direction nor the helots. - c. Soyinka's Introduction. "Dionysianism, essentially agrarian in origin" (p. vii, quoted in Quaicoe). The Introduction places the rite before Euripides. - The article does not cite it. It quotes the play twice (pp. 241, 263) and cites two more pages (234, 300). - It calls the play's tearing "the Euripidean sparagmos" (p. 27) and the sparagmos "that Euripides caused to be forever associated with" the Dionysiac (p. 20). - d. Morrison on the making of Greece. "Seventy years to eliminate Egypt as the cradle of civilization and its model and replace it with Greece. The triumph of that process was that Greece lost its own origins and became itself original" (Tanner, p. 130). "Canon building is empire building. Canon defense is national defense" (p. 132). - She quotes Bernal at length because in his research motive is located, delineated and confronted (pp. 131–132, in paraphrase). - The lecture is not in the article's references. Bernal enters the article as confirmation that African and European were "intermingled in antiquity too" (p. 9). - e. Morrison on Greek tragedy. Her satisfaction in reading Greek tragedy lies "in its similarity to Afro-American communal structures (the function of song and chorus, the heroic struggle between the claims of community and individual hubris) and African religion and philosophy." The form "makes available these varieties of provocative love because it is masterly, not because the civilization that is its referent was flawless or superior to all others" (Tanner, p. 125). - The receiving author keeps the form as kin and refuses the civilizational claim built on it. - The article's frame for her relation to the form is tearing. - f. Sula's first sentence. As revised and quoted by Morrison: "In that place, where they tore the nightshade and blackberry patches from their roots to make room for the Medallion City Golf Course, there was once a neighborhood" (Tanner, p. 152). The novel's first tearing is done by the town to the Black neighbourhood. - The article does not quote the sentence ("nightshade" 0, "golf" 0). - It assigns the novel's tearing to Morrison, acting on Ajax.

The count: of six points where the receiving works recognize the discipline's colonization, the article quotes one and turns it to another use (a). It reads a second without the recognition: the slave chorus is present and its Greek slaves are not (b). The other four are absent (c–f).

The six points are of two kinds, and each treatment is weighed for what admitting the point would change. - Points that confront the claims governing the discipline's object (a, d, e): Soyinka on the Greek origin of drama, Morrison on the making of Greece, and Morrison on the form held apart from the civilizational claim. - Admitted, each would move the article's explanation from the receiver's tearing of an inherited body to the discipline's earlier making of that body. - That is the move the article's own notes 16 and 46 make in small, and its argument does not. - Points that supply other evidence (b, c, f): Greek slavery restored to the Greek play, a rite older than Euripides, and the tearing done to a Black neighbourhood. - Admitted, (b) and (c) would date the play's materials before the Euripidean text the article treats as the play's source. (f) would give the novel's first act of tearing to the town and its developers, before any tearing of Ajax. - Each would change who tears what in the article's own model.

Morrison's lecture names the treatment the receiving works meet. Criticism has treated Afro-American work as "not so much 'art' as ore - rich ore" awaiting a Western smith (Tanner, p. 130), and judging it by Eurocentric criteria can "cripple the literature" (p. 134). On that account the article's measure (K3) is the smith's.

K3. The composed classic supplies the measure throughout: - Ogun is "a more developed, more complex kind of Dionysus" (p. 23). - Ajax remains the novel's "primary classical antecedent" (abstract), though the novel's Ajax "was never his name at all" (Sula, quoted p. 14) and Morrison says "Nothing is deliberate" (n. 17). - A rite Soyinka calls agrarian in origin is "Euripidean".

K4. In the body text the source is whole: "the ancient Ajax remains nowhere 'whole' within the novel" (p. 10). In the notes it is already composed: - Ajax "enacts a dismemberment and 're-membering' of the figure of Ajax as portrayed in the Iliad and Odyssey" (n. 16). - The Byzantine Christus Patiens "has enabled modern audiences to 'reconstitute' some of the missing sections from the end of Euripides' tragedy" (n. 46). - Zeitlin: "all Classics is 'reception'" (p. 4).

The Bacchae survives in two medieval manuscripts, L to line 755 and P for the whole play, so its last third rests on P alone. Its fragmentary ending is supplemented from the cento (Maribo, Aigis 18.1; to be checked against Dodds). The whole that Soyinka is said to tear is a gathering of limbs made by philology.

K5. The tearing is given to the creative artists (p. 5), and scholarship is set aside.

7.11.6.5 The finding. On all five criteria the article holds the composed classic against the receiving texts. - Colonial agency is given to users and its consequences to the texts. The apparatus that made the object appears only as an authority supplying the frame, and is never examined as an agent (K1). - The receiving works' recognitions of the discipline's colonization are redirected (two of six) or absent (four of six) (K2). - The classic measures the receivers (K3). - The source is whole in the argument and composed only in the notes (K4). - The tearing belongs to the receivers (K5).

The author's thesis inverts the metaphor at each point: - The sparagmos was the discipline's: loss, selection, a last third resting on one manuscript, an ending filled from a Christian cento, and an origin moved from Egypt to Greece. - The edition is its compositio membrorum. - The journal continues the gathering. Each article returns a scattered reception to the gathered body, the source column of §9.10. - The article's own word for that operation is communion, "in some senses the very opposite of sparagmos" (p. 25).

The receiving works hold the point of contact the field's premise asks for (§7.11.4). Soyinka disputes the origin, the play restores the helots, and Morrison names the seventy years and the empire in the canon. The flagship article of the field's editor reads past them.
